Study Overview

This study investigates whether Dartmouth students exhibit a predictable weekly cognitive rhythm — that is, whether attention and cognitive performance vary systematically across days of the week, time of day, and social behavior (e.g. sleep, going out). The session takes approximately 2–3 minutes and involves four short cognitive tasks.

Risk & Discomfort

This study involves minimal risk. You may experience mild eye strain from reading words on screen, particularly during the Stroop color-naming task. You are free to pause or stop at any time. There are no known psychological, physical, or social risks associated with participation.

Data Collection & Anonymity

  • No personally identifying information is collected (no name, email, or student ID).
  • Responses are submitted anonymously to a secure research spreadsheet.
  • Data will only be used for academic research purposes within this course.
  • You may download your own data at the end of the session.

Voluntary Participation

Participation is entirely voluntary. You may withdraw at any point without penalty. Completing or not completing this study has no effect on your academic standing.

Study Debrief
DEBRIEF

Study Purpose

Thank you for participating in the Cognitive Rhythm Study. The goal of this research is to determine whether Dartmouth students show systematic variation in cognitive performance across the days of the week — a "weekly cognitive rhythm" — and to identify what factors (sleep, social behavior, time of day, athletic schedule) best predict those fluctuations.

Now that you've completed the study, we can share what we were testing more specifically.

Research Hypotheses

H1 · Weekly Performance Rhythm We predicted that cognitive performance would be lower on days following high social activity (Friday–Saturday nights), particularly on Sundays and Mondays, and higher mid-week (Tuesday–Thursday) when social obligations are lower and sleep is more regular.
H2 · Sleep as Primary Mediator We hypothesized that sleep quantity the prior night would be the strongest single predictor of composite cognitive score — stronger than caffeine, time of day, or social behavior — and that this relationship would be especially pronounced for reaction time and memory recall.
H3 · Athlete vs. Non-Athlete Divergence We predicted that varsity athletes would show a distinct weekly rhythm compared to non-athletes, reflecting structured practice and competition schedules. Specifically, we expected athletes to show greater performance dips around game/practice days and stronger recovery patterns tied to their training cycles.

About the Tasks

  • Word Memorization & Recall: Measured short-term verbal memory and resistance to retroactive interference from intervening tasks.
  • Internal Timing: Measured temporal perception — the ability to track time without external cues. Fatigue and stimulants both distort this.
  • Reaction Time: A classic measure of simple alertness and neural processing speed, known to degrade with sleep loss.
  • Stroop Test: Measured cognitive inhibition and executive control — the ability to suppress automatic responses. One of the most widely used tools in cognitive psychology.

A Note on the Background Questions

Some questions in the survey were framed as measuring "current factors" (like environment or day of week) without specifying their role in the study. This was intentional — revealing the hypotheses beforehand could have influenced how you answered or performed. We appreciate your understanding.
